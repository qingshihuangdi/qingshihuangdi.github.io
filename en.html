02 — EXPERTISE

Expertise

Three standards address three different problems — things breaking, functions falling short, and behaviour learned from data. They belong in one coordinated safety framework, not in one blurred category.

FUNCTIONAL SAFETY

Functional Safety

ISO 26262:2018 · GB/T 34590-2022

Addresses random hardware failures and systematic failures: faults must be detected even when they are invisible, and the system must transition to a predefined safe state.

  • Safety requirements decomposition and ASIL allocation
  • System safety architecture and safety mechanisms
  • Safety development process, verification and confirmation
SOTIF

Safety of the Intended Functionality

ISO 21448:2022 · GB/T 43267-2023

Addresses insufficiencies of the intended functionality itself: nothing is broken, yet perception and decision-making can fall short in specific situations.

  • Functional insufficiency and triggering-condition analysis
  • ODD definition and scenario-based risk analysis
  • Reasonably foreseeable misuse analysis
AI SAFETY

AI Safety

ISO/PAS 8800:2024

Addresses behaviour learned from data: models fail on scenarios they have never seen, and can be confidently wrong when they have learned the wrong thing.

  • AI capability and limitation analysis
  • System-level safety constraints and safe integration
  • Engineering governance across data, models and training
Perspective: I assess AI from a systems-safety standpoint — where it will fail, and how it can be constrained — rather than developing AI algorithms.
03 — SELECTED PRACTICE

Selected Practice

Four programmes covering platform safety architecture, higher-level automated driving, verification capability, and team and management-system building.

Vehicle E/E Platform Safety ArchitecturePLATFORM SAFETY ARCHITECTURE

BackgroundThe corporate E/E architecture became platform-based, with several vehicle programmes sharing one platform.
ProblemIf every project does safety on its own, requirement wording diverges, architecture decisions conflict, and platform-level failure modes are owned by no one.
ApproachLed the functional safety and SOTIF requirements baseline and the top-level safety architecture for the platform, co-designed with the E/E architecture, with model-based analysis (medini analyze) supporting decomposition and traceability.
OutcomeA reusable platform safety baseline, with safety engineered in at the architecture stage instead of being retrofitted late in development.

L2+ / L3 / L4 Automated Driving SafetyAUTOMATED DRIVING SAFETY

BackgroundAdvanced driver assistance, L3 conditional-automation passenger cars and L4 unmanned logistics vehicles were developed in parallel.
ProblemDriving responsibility shifts from the human to the system: on failure the system must either maintain function or exit safely, and behaviour at ODD limits and under triggering conditions must be defined in advance.
ApproachLed the functional safety concepts and SOTIF analyses of these programmes and established a distributed, highly redundant vehicle architecture with full-stack fault-degradation strategies across sensors, domain controllers and actuators, distinguishing fail-safe from fail-operational where each applies.
OutcomeSafety concepts delivered for several higher-level automated driving programmes, providing the safety input for market-entry compliance.

Safety Development & Verification CapabilityVERIFICATION CAPABILITY

BackgroundHowever complete a safety analysis is on paper, without verification means its conclusions remain paper claims.
ProblemWhether safety mechanisms actually act as designed under fault injection requires physical evidence, not review signatures.
ApproachBuilt in-house in-vehicle and E/E bench functional-safety fault-injection verification and confirmation capability, closing the loop from requirements through analysis and design to verification.
OutcomeFault injection became a repeatable engineering capability, and the effectiveness of safety mechanisms is backed by measured evidence.

Safety Team & Management System BuildingTEAM & MANAGEMENT SYSTEM

BackgroundWhen I joined, the company had no dedicated functional safety team or system; the work was scattered across development departments.
ProblemWithout organisation, processes and review mechanisms, safety work depends on individual experience and cannot support parallel programmes or external audits.
ApproachBuilt the safety team from 0 to 1 with a safety technology matrix, coordinated functional safety and SOTIF development activities, and established work-product standards, review mechanisms and an audit-and-correction loop.
OutcomeThe functional safety management system was certified by TÜV Rheinland (ISO 26262) and the SOTIF management system by SGS (ISO 21448); several programmes achieved safety compliance for domestic and overseas markets. Certification of the AI safety management system by TÜV Rheinland is in progress.

05 — APPROACH

Approach

Knowing the standards is only the starting point. The real work is translating their requirements into architecture, processes and verifiable engineering evidence.

STEP 1

Understand

Understand the standard's requirements, the product's functions, its operating scenarios and the system boundary — define the problem correctly first.

STEP 2

Architect

Establish the safety concept: requirements decomposition, system architecture and safety mechanism design.

STEP 3

Integrate

Embed safety activities into development processes, organisational responsibilities and work products — not as an external check bolted on afterwards.

STEP 4

Verify

Close the verification and confirmation loop through analysis, testing and fault injection, so that conclusions rest on evidence.
